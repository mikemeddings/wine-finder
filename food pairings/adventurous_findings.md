# Adventurous wine pairings: findings from two books

**Sources**

- **H** = Robert J. Harrington, *Food and Wine Pairing: A Sensory Experience* (Wiley, 2008). Cited as **H pr.N**, meaning printed page N. The extracted text file is offset by 17 pages: file page = printed page + 17. That offset was verified against the page markers.
- **D** = Andrew Dornenburg & Karen Page, *What to Drink with What You Eat* (Bulfinch, 2006). Cited by chapter and entry heading, plus the expert quoted where there is one. For example, **D Ch5 "SALMON" (Johnnes)**.
  - Ch3 = "Food and Beverage Pairing 101"
  - Ch4 = "Selecting and Serving"
  - Ch5 = food-to-drink A–Z
  - Ch6 = drink-to-food A–Z
  - Ch7 = restaurant menus
  - Ch8 = desert-island lists

**Typography key in D**

D's A–Z lists use type styles to show strength:

- plain = recommended by at least one expert
- **bold** = frequently recommended
- CAPS = very highly recommended
- \*CAPS = classic
- AVOID lists = things the experts warn against

Below, CAPS mean the book printed the entry in caps.

**What counts as "adventurous" here**

A pairing counts as adventurous if it falls *outside* what the app already treats as classic for that food:

| Food | Classic in the app |
|---|---|
| Red meat, lamb | full/firm reds |
| Fish, seafood | light–medium whites, rosé, dry sparkling |
| Pork, pasta/pizza | reds that are not both full and firm; whites that are not light |
| Chicken | light reds, all whites |
| Cheese | full/firm reds, full whites, sweet wines |
| Spicy food | off-dry/sweet whites, fruity soft reds, rosé |
| Dessert | sweet, dessert and fortified wines |

**Already sourced before this pass** (re-confirmed below where the books support them):

- light soft reds with fish
- Pinot Noir with shellfish
- full oaky whites with steak (support for this one turned out to be **weak**; see Red meat and Cautions)

Quotes are kept under 15 words and were checked word for word against the source text before inclusion.

## 1. Fish

### Light and low-tannin reds with fish (confirms the existing rule, with detail)

- **Oregon Pinot Noir with wild salmon, grilled or over open fire.**
  - Why: higher acid and low tannin suit the fat and texture of the salmon.
  - Source: H pr.85 (Table 4.1).
  - Quote: "higher acidity and lower tannin in these cool-climate Pinots".
  - Also H pr.60 (Table 3.2).
- **Low-tannin reds with fish in general: Pinot Noir, Beaujolais, Dolcetto, even Zinfandel.**
  - Why: wood and tannin are what make fish taste metallic, so pick reds without them.
  - Source: H pr.155.
  - Quote: "Pinot Noir, Beaujolais, Dolcetto, or even Zinfandel".
- **Lemberger (Blaufränkisch) or Beaujolais with cedar-planked trout and asparagus.**
  - Why: fruit and mild tannin worked with the acidity of the asparagus.
  - Source: H pr.244.
  - Quote: "mild tannins worked well with the natural acidity in the asparagus".
- **Pinot Noir, or "even Merlot", with grilled salmon.**
  - Why: grilling gives the salmon a bigger, meatier taste that "begs for a more substantial wine".
  - Source: H pr.172.
  - Quote: "a Pinot Noir or even Merlot might also be appropriate".
- **A light red Burgundy even with white fish; Pinot Noir with heavier fish such as salmon.**
  - Why: weight matters more than colour.
  - Source: D Ch3 Rule #2 (Bernard Sun).
  - Quote: "A light Pinot Noir from Burgundy will even work with a white fish."
- **The book lists reds as standard fish wines.**
  - D Ch5 "FISH" lists Bardolino, Beaujolais, red Burgundy, Pinot Noir and Rioja alongside the whites.
  - D Ch6 "LIGHT-BODIED RED WINE" lists fish and salmon as matches.
  - D Ch6 "LOW-TANNIN WINE" (Barbera, Beaujolais, Dolcetto, Pinot, Valpolicella) also lists fish.

### Light reds with beef (new)

- **Beaujolais with charred beef filet salad with garlic vinaigrette.**
  - Why: acid matches the olive oil; body matches; smoke and fruit contrast.
  - Source: H pr.257 and pr.260. Scored 8/10, and the predicted score matched the tasted score.
- **Gamay or young red Burgundy with prime rib.**
  - Source: D Ch5 "BEEF: prime rib".
- **Beaujolais, Gamay or Merlot with well-done steak.**
  - Why: well-done steak loses fat, which makes tannin harsher.
  - Source: D Ch5 "STEAK: medium-well to well-done".
- **Beaujolais with steak frites and cold sliced steak.**
  - Source: D Ch6 "BEAUJOLAIS"; D Ch5 "STEAK: cold sliced".
- **Pinot Noir with heartier dishes: tuna, game, steak.**
  - Source: D Ch8 Duncan.
  - Quote: "People don't realize how well Pinot Noir works with heartier dishes."

### Note on light whites with pork (a classic in the books)

Both books treat **light, high-acid Riesling with pork** as a *classic*:

- H pr.83: acidity contrasts the fat in pork.
- D Ch5 "PORK" lists RIESLING in caps, and Kabinett/Spätlese with roast pork.

The app's rule (whites that are not light) would wrongly tag this as adventurous. See Contradictions.

## Contradictions with the app's current rules

1. **Cheese.**
   - The app treats full/firm reds as *classic* with cheese.
   - Both books say the opposite is closer to the truth:
     - Whites rated best across cheeses (H pr.272).
     - Whites "usually go best" (H pr.239).
     - Tannins clash with cheese (D Ch5 "CHEESE", McCalman; D Ch3, Boulud).
     - Grilled Brie with Cabernet scored 3.5/10 (H pr.257).
     - The most cheese-friendly red in Harrington's research is Pinot Noir, which is *not* full/firm.
   - Suggestion: demote full/firm reds to "conditional", favouring hard, aged or cow's-milk cheeses (Parmesan or Pecorino with Barolo or Brunello; aged cheddar).
   - Suggestion: treat crisp, aromatic and off-dry whites (Sancerre/Sauvignon Blanc with goat; Gewürztraminer; Riesling) and Champagne with soft-ripened cheese as classic.
2. **Pork with light whites.**
   - Light, high-acid Riesling with pork is a *classic* in both books (H pr.83, pr.85; D Ch5 "PORK").
   - The app's "whites that aren't light" rule would wrongly tag it as adventurous.
3. **Light red with fish.**
   - Pinot Noir with salmon is now treated as the obvious match.
   - Source: D Ch5 "SALMON" (Johnnes: "so stereotypical and so obvious").
   - H pr.85 lists it among regional classics.
   - Suggestion: keep Pinot with *white* fish as adventurous, but Pinot or Gamay with salmon and tuna is mainstream.
4. **Full oaky white with steak (an existing adventurous example).**
   - Support for this example is weak.
   - The only direct support is Renaud's grudging white Burgundy ("not that I'd recommend it", D Ch5 "STEAK").
   - D Ch6 "CHARDONNAY" says AVOID red meat.
   - Better-supported versions:
     - Chardonnay with steak with Béarnaise (D Ch5 "STEAK").
     - Condrieu with filet with citrus hollandaise (D Ch6 "CONDRIEU").
     - Off-dry or high-acid Riesling with raw or cold beef (D Preface; H pr.85).
     - Rosé Champagne with rare beef.
5. **Pinot Noir with shellfish (an existing adventurous example).**
   - It is supported for lobster, soy-glazed scallops and mushroom-sauced seafood.
   - But D Ch5 "SHRIMP", "SCALLOPS", "OYSTERS", "SNAILS" and "LOBSTER" all carry "AVOID red wine".
   - Suggestion: restrict it to lobster, crab and crayfish, and to tomato-, mushroom-, soy- or bacon-led preparations. Do not use it for raw oysters, plain shrimp or plain scallops.
6. **Rosé and sparkling with seafood (classic in the app).**
   - D Ch6 "ROSÉ" says AVOID raw oysters.
   - Spellman finds Champagne with oysters metallic (D Ch5 "OYSTERS").
   - Both are minority or specific views, but the app's blanket "classic" overstates it for raw oysters.
7. **Pasta and pizza.**
   - The app's "reds that aren't full and firm" includes Chianti.
   - Greg Harrington calls Chianti with American pizza "the worst pairing" because the pizza is sweet.
   - D Ch5 "PASTA: tomato sauce" says AVOID tannic red.
   - Suggestion: prefer low-tannin, fruity reds (Barbera, Dolcetto, Montepulciano, fruity Zinfandel) for sweet tomato sauces.
8. **Fruity soft reds with spicy food (classic in the app).**
   - Broadly supported (Beaujolais, Gamay, lighter Zinfandel, chilled).
   - But D Ch6 "PINOT NOIR" says AVOID spicy dishes.
   - Keep the rule, but cap it at medium heat.

## Principles that explain why (Harrington's 12 rules, H pr.230, plus key mechanisms)

1. **Sweetness:** food sweetness must not exceed wine sweetness. Otherwise the wine tastes thin and sour (H pr.104–106). This is why dry sparkling with wedding cake fails, and why savoury dishes with a sweet element want an off-dry wine.
2. **Acidity:** food acidity must not exceed wine acidity. High-acid food with high-acid wine cancel out, and the wine's fruit shows (H pr.109). Examples: goat cheese with Sancerre; vinaigrette salad with Beaujolais.
3. **Salt and bubbles:** salty foods suit high effervescence (H pr.136). Salt magnifies bitterness, tannin and alcohol heat (H pr.132). This explains Champagne with fried and salty food, and sherry with fried chicken.
4. **Bitterness and bubbles:** bitterness is lessened by bubbles and masked by sweetness (H pr.136–137). Bitter food with bitter wine compounds (H pr.132–133). In D, some experts compare bitter food with *moderately* tannic wine (grilled eggplant, walnuts, charred steak; D Ch3, Rosengarten).
5. **Tannin = animal fat:** fat and protein soften tannin (H pr.172). This explains big reds with fatty steak and lamb, and Cahors with cassoulet. It also explains why well-done (lean) steak and lean fish make tannic reds harsh.
6. **Acidity = vegetable fat:** olive oil and plant fats want high-acid wines (H pr.172). Dairy fat is better matched by body (oaked Chardonnay with butter or cream sauces; H pr.110–111).
7. **Body = body:** match weight (H pr.172; D Ch3 Rule #2). Salmon and tuna are heavy enough for Pinot, Merlot or even Zinfandel. Robust cooking (grilling, roasting) makes any food more red-friendly (H pr.85, pr.171).
8. **Spiciness = spiciness:** peppery reds (Shiraz, Zinfandel, Côtes du Rhône) with peppery and savoury spice (H pr.198, pr.200).
9. **Hot spice wants off-dry, acidic, low-alcohol whites.** Tannin, alcohol and oak amplify heat (H pr.193, pr.198). The exceptions are ripe, fruit-forward New World reds with moderate spice (H pr.200).
10. **Flavour type** can match by similarity or contrast (H pr.215). Examples: smoky food with fruity Riesling; earthy food with Old World wine.
11. **Flavour intensity** should be equal (H pr.215).
12. **Flavour persistency** should be equal (H pr.217).

**Other mechanisms used above**

- **Iodine plus tannin.** Tannin reacts with the iodine in seafood and tastes metallic (D Ch3 sidebar, Sun). This is why shellfish is the strongest "no red" zone.
- **Cheese and red wine.** Cheese fat and protein soften wine (H pr.270). But tannin clashes with many cheeses, and blue cheese mutes white-wine flavours (H pr.216, pr.272; D Ch5 "CHEESE", McCalman).
- **Contrast pairings.** These make one partner a "supporting actor" rather than a synergy (H pr.146, pr.158).
